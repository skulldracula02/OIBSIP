Marie Curie

A pioneering scientist who revealed the hidden power of radioactivity.

Biography & Legacy

Born Maria Sk2odowska in 1867, Marie Curie became one of the most influential scientists in history. She moved from Poland to Paris to study physics and mathematics, overcoming language barriers and financial hardship to pursue her passion for research.

Curie's commitment to science led her to discover two new elements, polonium and radium, while working in a makeshift laboratory. Her detailed studies of radiation established the field of atomic physics and laid the foundation for modern cancer treatments.

In addition to winning the Nobel Prize in Physics with her husband Pierre Curie, Marie received a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry, making her the only person to earn Nobel honors in two different scientific fields. Her legacy includes the first mobile X-ray units used during World War I.

Marie Curie's work transformed how scientists understand the invisible forces of nature. Her courage, intellectual rigor, and dedication to public service continue to inspire students, researchers, and advocates for women in STEM.

Key Achievements

1898

Discovery of Polonium and Radium

Curie and Pierre announced the discovery of two new elements, establishing a new branch of physical chemistry.

1903

Nobel Prize in Physics

Marie shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for their research on radioactivity.

1911

Nobel Prize in Chemistry

Curie received her second Nobel Prize for isolating pure radium and advancing the scientific understanding of radioactive materials.

1914–1918

Mobile X-ray Units

During World War I, Curie organized mobile radiography centers to help surgeons treat wounded soldiers near the front lines.
